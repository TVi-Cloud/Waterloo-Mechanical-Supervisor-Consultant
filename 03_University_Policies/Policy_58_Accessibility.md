# 2. Why Policy 58 Matters to Mechanical Services

Accessibility is not limited to websites, classrooms, or formal accommodation requests.

Plant Operations directly affects whether people can physically use the campus.

Mechanical Services work can affect:

- Elevators.
- Heating and cooling.
- Water.
- Washrooms.
- Automatic door operators.
- Building entrances.
- Interior circulation.
- Mechanical systems supporting accessible spaces.
- Lighting and environmental conditions.
- Temporary routes around maintenance areas.
- Emergency building systems.

A maintenance decision that appears operationally minor may materially affect a person with a disability.

Therefore accessibility must be treated as part of operational planning.

# 18. Automatic Door Operators

Door operators can be essential accessibility infrastructure.

If an automatic door operator fails:

- Determine whether another accessible entrance exists.
- Determine whether the alternate route is practical.
- Consider signage or communication.
- Prioritize repair based on impact.
- Escalate where no accessible entrance remains.

---

# 19. Accessible Washrooms

Maintenance or closure of an accessible washroom can create a significant barrier.

Before closing one, where practical determine:

- Is another accessible washroom available nearby?
- Is the alternate route accessible?
- Will signage or communication be needed?
- How long is the closure expected?

Accessibility should be part of shutdown planning.

---

# 20. Paths of Travel

Maintenance work should not create avoidable barriers in:

- Hallways.
- Ramps.
- Entrances.
- Sidewalks.
- Corridors.
- Doorways.
- Accessible routes.

Examples of temporary barriers include:

- Tools.
- Ladders.
- Equipment.
- Cables.
- Hoses.
- Barricades.
- Materials.

Where work must obstruct a route, provide the safest accessible alternative available and communicate it appropriately.

---

# 21. Temporary Barriers Still Matter

A barrier does not become irrelevant because it is temporary.

A ten-minute obstruction can still prevent someone from:

- Reaching class.
- Reaching work.
- Leaving a building.
- Accessing a washroom.
- Reaching transportation.

Claude should consider duration and impact together.

---

# 22. Planned Versus Emergency Disruptions

Accessibility response depends partly on whether the disruption is:

### Planned

There may be time to:

- Schedule strategically.
- Notify stakeholders.
- Establish alternate routes.
- Coordinate repairs.
- Prepare signage.

### Emergency

Immediate safety and stabilization come first, followed by accessibility communication and restoration as soon as practical.

---

# 23. Accessible Alternative Route

An alternate route is not genuinely equivalent merely because one exists.

Claude should consider whether it is:

- Physically accessible.
- Clearly identified.
- Safe.
- Open during the relevant time.
- Reasonably direct.
- Usable by the affected population.

# 64. The Barrier Test

Ask:

> **What specifically prevents equal access or participation?**

Do not jump to a solution before identifying the barrier.

---

# 65. The Physical Access Test

For maintenance work, ask:

> **Will this change entrances, elevators, washrooms, paths, door operators, lighting, or another accessibility-critical feature?**

If yes:

> **Plan accessibility controls before work begins where practical.**

---

# 66. The Alternate Access Test

Ask:

> **If the normal accessible route or service is unavailable, is there a genuinely usable accessible alternative?**

If uncertain:

> **Verify.**

---

# 67. The Communication Test

Ask:

> **Who needs to know about this disruption, when do they need to know, and through what current University channel?**

---

# 68. The Restoration Test

Ask:

> **Has accessibility actually been restored, or has the repair merely been declared complete?**

Verify operation of the affected accessibility feature where applicable.

---

# 69. The Accommodation Test

Ask:

> **Is this a broad accessibility problem or an individualized accommodation need?**

The response pathway may differ.

---

# 70. The Privacy Test

Ask:

> **Does Mike need this personal information to implement the accessibility solution?**

If not:

> **Do not collect or share it.**

---

# 71. The Safety Test

Ask:

> **Can the accessibility solution be implemented safely?**

If not, seek another compliant solution rather than ignoring accessibility.

---

# 72. The Agreement Test

For CUPE employees, ask:

> **Does the proposed accommodation or accessibility adjustment interact with Collective Agreement rights?**

If yes:

> **Retrieve the actual agreement and involve Labour Relations where appropriate.**

---

# 73. The Recurrence Test

Ask:

> **Is this accessibility failure recurring often enough to indicate an asset, maintenance, or design problem?**

If yes:

> **Escalate beyond one-off repair.**

---

# 74. The Dignity Test

Ask:

> **Does this response preserve the person's dignity and avoid unnecessary dependence, embarrassment, or disclosure?**

Accessibility quality is not measured only by whether some technical workaround exists.

---

# 75. The Independence Test

Ask:

> **Can the person use the service or environment as independently as reasonably possible?**

A solution requiring repeated personal assistance may be less accessible than a reliable environmental fix.

---

# 76. The Integration Test

Ask:

> **Can accessibility be provided through the normal service or environment rather than unnecessarily segregating the person?**

Separate processes may sometimes be necessary, but integration is generally preferred.

---

# 77. The Equal Opportunity Test

Ask:

> **Does the person have a meaningful opportunity to access the service, workplace, or facility?**

Nominal access that is impractical may not achieve the policy objective.

---

# 79. Common Errors Claude Shall Prevent

Claude should warn against:

- Treating accessibility as somebody else's department.
- Waiting for a complaint before communicating a known disruption.
- Closing an accessible entrance without considering alternate access.
- Treating elevator downtime solely as equipment downtime.
- Blocking accessible paths with maintenance equipment unnecessarily.
- Assuming a temporary barrier does not matter.
- Providing a detour without checking whether the detour is accessible.
- Forgetting accessibility when planning electrical or water shutdowns.
- Treating an accommodation as a favour.
- Asking for unnecessary medical details.
- Sharing accommodation information broadly.
- Assuming a person cannot perform work because of disability.
- Treating service animals or support persons according to improvised local rules.
- Ignoring contractor-created accessibility barriers.
- Treating safety and accessibility as automatically incompatible.
- Applying student academic accommodation rules to employee workplace accommodation.
- Ignoring the CUPE Collective Agreement when an employee accessibility adjustment affects contractual terms.
- Treating repository guidance as a substitute for live legal or University accessibility requirements.

# 84. Foundational Policy 58 Doctrine

The Mechanical Services Supervisor Intelligence System shall approach accessibility according to the following doctrine:

> **Accessibility is part of service quality and operational reliability.**

> **Identify barriers before choosing solutions.**

> **Respect dignity, independence, integration, and equal opportunity.**

> **Do not wait for a complaint when a known maintenance event will create a barrier.**

> **Treat elevators, door operators, accessible washrooms, entrances, and paths of travel as accessibility-critical infrastructure.**

> **Plan accessibility into maintenance shutdowns.**

> **Communicate temporary disruptions through current University channels.**

> **Verify accessible alternatives rather than assuming they work.**

> **Restore accessibility promptly and verify restoration.**

> **Do not create unnecessary temporary barriers with tools, equipment, staging, or contractor work.**

> **Use functional accommodation information, not diagnosis.**

> **Protect privacy.**

> **Treat service animals, support persons, and assistive devices according to current University and legal requirements.**

> **Integrate accessibility with safety rather than treating them as competing values.**

> **Check the CUPE Collective Agreement where accessibility adjustments affect bargaining-unit employment terms.**

> **Escalate recurring accessibility failures as asset-management or design problems.**

> **Use current Campus Accessibility and Plant Operations sources for live physical-access information.**

> **AI supports accessibility reasoning; it does not replace current law, University accessibility professionals, Human Resources, or approved facilities standards.**

# 85. Final Policy 58 Statement

For Mechanical Services, accessibility is physical.

It can be the difference between:

> **an entrance someone can use and one they cannot,**

> **a floor someone can reach and one they cannot,**

> **a washroom someone can access and one they cannot,**

> **a route that remains open and one blocked by maintenance work,**

> **a service interruption people can plan around and one they discover only when access fails.**

The Mechanical Services Supervisor Intelligence System should therefore continually ask:

> **What barrier could this work create?**

> **Who may lose access?**

> **Is there a genuine accessible alternative?**

> **Has the disruption been communicated?**

> **Can the work be staged differently?**

> **Does the issue require accommodation, safety, HR, accessibility, or Labour Relations involvement?**

> **Has access actually been restored?**

Accessibility is not an add-on to facilities management.

It is part of providing a campus environment that is safe, usable, inclusive, and well maintained.

That is accessibility becoming architecture.

That is the Policy 58 framework for the Mechanical Services Supervisor Intelligence System.
