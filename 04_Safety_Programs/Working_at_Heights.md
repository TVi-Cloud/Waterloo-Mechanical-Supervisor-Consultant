# 2. Core Principle

The foundational rule:

> **Any work where a person could fall and be injured requires deliberate hazard assessment and appropriate controls before work begins.**

The goal is not simply preventing a fall.

The goal is preventing a worker from ever being exposed to an uncontrolled fall hazard.

---

# 3. Fall Hazard Mindset

A supervisor should ask:

> "What happens if this person loses balance, slips, trips, or the equipment fails?"

If the answer is serious injury:

> The hazard requires control.

---

# 4. Supervisor Responsibility

Mike's responsibilities include:

- Identifying fall hazards.
- Ensuring proper controls are selected.
- Confirming workers are trained.
- Ensuring equipment is appropriate.
- Inspecting conditions.
- Stopping unsafe work.
- Ensuring rescue considerations exist.

---

# 5. Hazard Assessment

Before elevated work, consider:

- Height.
- Surface stability.
- Edge exposure.
- Openings.
- Equipment condition.
- Weather/environment.
- Worker competence.
- Tools/materials.
- Access and egress.
- Rescue requirements.

---

# 6. Hierarchy of Fall Protection

Preferred controls:

1. Eliminate work at height.
2. Prevent access to fall hazards.
3. Install engineering controls.
4. Use approved fall protection systems.
5. Use administrative controls where appropriate.

PPE is important, but it is not the first solution when better controls exist.

---

# 7. Elimination and Engineering Controls

Examples:

- Performing work from ground level.
- Installing permanent platforms.
- Adding guardrails.
- Designing safer access.
- Using remote tools.

The safest fall protection system is often the one that prevents exposure entirely.

---

# 8. Guardrails

Guardrails are a passive protection system.

Advantages:

- Does not depend on worker action.
- Protects multiple people.
- Provides continuous protection.

Where practical, passive protection is preferred.

---

# 9. Personal Fall Protection

Personal systems may include:

- Full-body harnesses.
- Lifelines.
- Lanyards.
- Anchor points.
- Self-retracting lifelines.

Equipment must be:

- Appropriate.
- Inspected.
- Compatible.
- Used correctly.

---

# 10. Training and Competence

Workers performing work at heights require appropriate training.

Training must include:

- Recognition of fall hazards.
- Proper equipment use.
- Inspection.
- Limitations.
- Emergency response.
- Rescue awareness.

A certificate does not automatically prove competence.

---

# 11. Competency Model

Use:

> READ → WATCH → PERFORM → VERIFY

READ:
- Procedure.
- Hazard assessment.
- Equipment instructions.

WATCH:
- Competent demonstration.

PERFORM:
- Worker performs task safely.

VERIFY:
- Supervisor confirms competence.

---

# 12. Equipment Inspection

Before use, inspect equipment for:

- Damage.
- Wear.
- Defects.
- Missing components.
- Improper condition.

Damaged equipment must be removed from service.

---

# 13. Harness Awareness

A harness is not a magic safety device.

A complete system requires:

- Proper fit.
- Suitable anchor.
- Correct connection.
- Fall clearance.
- Rescue plan.

---

# 14. Fall Clearance

Before using fall arrest:

Consider:

- Fall distance.
- Lanyard length.
- Deceleration distance.
- Worker height.
- Equipment stretch.
- Obstructions below.

A fall arrest system that allows a worker to strike an object is not adequate protection.

---

# 15. Suspension Trauma

After a fall arrest event, a suspended worker may experience serious medical consequences.

A rescue plan must consider:

- Speed of rescue.
- Communication.
- Equipment.
- Emergency response.

---

# 16. Rescue Planning

Before work involving fall arrest:

Ask:

- How will a fallen worker be recovered?
- Who performs rescue?
- What equipment exists?
- How quickly can help arrive?

A rescue plan cannot be invented after the fall occurs.

---

# 17. Ladders

Ladders should be selected based on the task.

Consider:

- Is a ladder the right tool?
- Duration of work.
- Worker position.
- Stability.
- Need for both hands.
- Fall consequences.

A ladder is not automatically a work platform.

---

# 23. Stop Work Triggers

Stop elevated work if:

- Proper equipment is unavailable.
- Worker training is uncertain.
- Weather creates unsafe conditions.
- Fall protection cannot be properly used.
- Rescue planning is absent.
- Equipment is damaged.
- Conditions change.

---

# 24. Common Errors Claude Shall Prevent

Claude should warn against:

- Using a ladder because it is faster.
- Working from unsafe surfaces.
- Skipping inspections.
- Assuming experienced workers do not need controls.
- Using damaged equipment.
- Forgetting rescue planning.
- Treating fall protection as optional.
- Allowing production pressure to override safety.
- Assuming training equals competence.

# 25. Supervisor Decision Framework

When Mike receives a working-at-heights question:

## Identify

What is the fall hazard?

## Assess

What could happen?

## Control

Can exposure be eliminated or reduced?

## Verify

Are controls in place?

## Confirm

Are workers trained and competent?

## Monitor

Have conditions changed?

# 26. Foundational Working-at-Heights Doctrine

> **Gravity never takes a day off.**

> **Every fall hazard deserves respect.**

> **The best fall protection is preventing exposure.**

> **Equipment does not protect workers unless used correctly.**

> **Training must become demonstrated competence.**

> **Rescue planning happens before the fall, not after it.**

> **Shortcuts at height can create permanent consequences.**

> **No deadline is worth a preventable fall.**

---

# 27. Final Statement

Working at heights represents the responsibility of controlling one of the oldest workplace hazards: gravity.

Mike's questions should be:

> **Can this work be done from the ground?**

> **What prevents the fall?**

> **What happens if the control fails?**

> **Is the worker trained and competent?**

> **How do we rescue someone if something goes wrong?**

> **Is this the safest way to complete the task?**

Safety becomes architecture when the system prevents the fall before the fall occurs.
