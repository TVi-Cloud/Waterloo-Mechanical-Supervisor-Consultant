# 2. Core Principle

The foundational rule:

> **Hot work is controlled fire creation. It is only safe when the fire hazards around it are intentionally managed.**

The question is not:

> "Can someone perform the task?"

The question is:

> "Can the task be performed while controlling ignition sources, fuel sources, people, and the environment?"

---

# 3. What Is Hot Work?

Hot work includes activities capable of producing:

- Flame.
- Heat.
- Sparks.
- Molten metal.
- Ignition sources.

Examples:

- Welding.
- Cutting.
- Brazing.
- Soldering.
- Torch-applied work.
- Grinding.
- Heating with open flame.

---

# 4. Why Hot Work Is High Risk

Hot work can create:

- Fire.
- Explosion.
- Smoke.
- Toxic fumes.
- Burns.
- Eye injury.
- Property damage.
- Hidden ignition.

A fire may begin after the work appears complete.

---

# 5. Supervisor Responsibility

Mike's responsibilities include ensuring:

- Hazards are assessed.
- Required controls are in place.
- Workers are trained.
- Required permits/processes are followed.
- Combustibles are controlled.
- Fire protection is considered.
- Contractors are coordinated.
- Unsafe work is stopped.

---

# 6. Hot Work Decision Process

Before hot work begins:

1. Identify the task.
2. Determine whether it is hot work.
3. Assess fire/explosion hazards.
4. Remove or protect combustibles.
5. Verify required controls.
6. Obtain required authorization.
7. Perform work safely.
8. Monitor after completion.

---

# 7. Hazard Assessment

Consider:

- Nearby combustibles.
- Flammable liquids or gases.
- Dusts.
- Hidden spaces.
- Building systems.
- Ventilation.
- Occupants.
- Fire detection systems.
- Sprinklers.
- Emergency access.

---

# 8. The Fuel-Ignition-Control Model

A fire requires:

- Fuel.
- Oxygen.
- Ignition source.

Hot work creates the ignition source.

The supervisor's job is to control the other conditions that allow ignition.

---

# 9. Combustible Materials

Before hot work:

Remove or protect:

- Paper.
- Wood.
- Packaging.
- Insulation.
- Dust.
- Chemicals.
- Flammable liquids.
- Other combustible materials.

Do not assume a small spark cannot travel.

---

# 10. Hidden Hazards

Hot work hazards may exist beyond the immediate work area.

Consider:

- Opposite side of walls.
- Above ceilings.
- Below floors.
- Ductwork.
- Pipe chases.
- Mechanical rooms.
- Adjacent spaces.

Heat and sparks can travel.

---

# 11. Fire Protection

Before work begins verify:

- Appropriate fire extinguishers available.
- Fire protection systems are operational.
- Emergency access is maintained.
- Required notifications are completed.

---

# 12. Hot Work Permit Concept

Where required by Waterloo procedure, a hot-work permit confirms that:

- The work was assessed.
- Hazards were reviewed.
- Controls were established.
- Authorization was given.

A permit is not a replacement for safe work.

---

# 13. Fire Watch

A fire watch may be required when hot work creates a risk of ignition.

A fire watch must understand:

- What hazards to monitor.
- How long monitoring is required.
- How to respond to fire.
- When to stop work.

---

# 14. Fire Watch Is Active Responsibility

A fire watch is not:

- Someone casually standing nearby.
- Someone doing another job.
- Someone watching their phone.

The role exists to detect and respond to fire hazards.

---

# 15. Post-Work Monitoring

Fire hazards may remain after hot work stops.

Consider:

- Smoldering materials.
- Heat transfer.
- Hidden ignition.
- Hot surfaces.

Follow required post-work monitoring periods.

---

Contractor hot work requires coordination.

# 20. Stop Work Triggers

Stop hot work if:

- Combustibles cannot be controlled.
- Required authorization is missing.
- Fire protection is unavailable.
- Atmospheric conditions change.
- Equipment is defective.
- Required PPE is missing.
- Workers are not trained.

---

# 21. Incident Response

If fire, injury, or dangerous conditions occur:

1. Stop work.
2. Protect people.
3. Activate emergency response if required.
4. Control hazards only if safe.
5. Report and investigate.

# 23. Common Errors Claude Shall Prevent

Claude should warn against:

- Treating grinding as harmless.
- Assuming small sparks cannot start fires.
- Ignoring adjacent areas.
- Skipping permits where required.
- Using a fire watch as a formality.
- Leaving combustibles nearby.
- Performing hot work around flammables.
- Ignoring ventilation.
- Restarting normal operations without confirming safety.
- Assuming contractor responsibility eliminates supervisor responsibility.

---

# 24. Foundational Hot Work Doctrine

> **Hot work creates controlled danger.**

> **The safest hot work is planned hot work.**

> **Remove fuel before creating ignition.**

> **Control the environment before controlling the tool.**

> **Authorization does not replace hazard control.**

> **A fire watch is a safety role, not a spectator role.**

> **The absence of fire during the task does not prove the process was safe.**

> **Hidden hazards are still hazards.**

> **Production pressure never justifies uncontrolled ignition risk.**

---

# 25. Final Statement

Hot work represents the principle of responsible power:

- The worker controls the flame.
- The supervisor controls the system.
- The organization controls the conditions.

Mike's questions should always be:

> **What can ignite?**

> **Where can heat or sparks travel?**

> **What controls prevent fire?**

> **Who is responsible for monitoring?**

> **What happens if something goes wrong?**

> **Are we confident enough to begin?**

That is hot work safety becoming architecture.
