# 5. HSEMS Design

Waterloo states that its HSEMS is designed using:

- CSA Z45001 occupational health-and-safety management-system principles.
- Ontario Occupational Health and Safety Act requirements.
- Applicable regulations.

The system is integrated into Waterloo's:

- Governance.
- Management.
- Operations.

Safety is therefore intended to be part of ordinary management rather than a separate parallel activity.

---

# 6. What the HSEMS Does

The current Waterloo HSEMS framework supports health, safety, and environment management by:

- Providing centralized HSE programs, standards, and services.
- Guiding departments in program implementation.
- Monitoring and auditing HSE programs.
- Supporting HSE committees.
- Maintaining Policy 34.
- Integrating HSE into governance.
- Supporting legislative compliance.

# 7. The Internal Responsibility System

The HSEMS is built around the Internal Responsibility System.

Every workplace party has responsibility for:

- Their own safety.
- The safety impact of their actions.
- Cooperating to identify hazards.
- Helping resolve hazards.
- Participating in HSE programs.

However, responsibilities increase with authority.

---

# 8. Supervisory Responsibility Is Greater

The HSEMS states that people in authority and supervisory roles must make HSE an integral part of their job.

Mike therefore cannot treat health and safety as:

> **something handled only by the Safety Office or Health and Safety Coordinator.**

It is part of his supervisory function.

# 10. Department Head Responsibility

- Maintaining awareness of applicable legislation and procedures.
- Implementing risk-identification and control processes.
- Ensuring incidents and near misses are investigated.

- Maintaining records.

Mike reports into this departmental safety structure.

# 11. Mike's HSEMS Supervisor Duties

The current HSEMS states that every person in a supervisory position is responsible for the health and safety of employees or students in their line of authority.

Mike's core HSEMS duties include:

- Taking every reasonable precaution.
- Ensuring work follows required procedures and controls.
- Ensuring required protective equipment and devices are used.
- Advising workers of actual or potential dangers.
- Identifying job-specific training.
- Ensuring training is provided.
- Maintaining job-specific training records.
- Completing required supervisor safety training.
- Promptly reporting and investigating incidents.
- Implementing corrective action.
- Escalating significant unresolved issues.

# 16. Planning Comes Before Control

The HSEMS requires departments to establish planning processes to:

- Identify hazards.
- Assess risks.
- Address risks and opportunities.

For Mike, this means safety should be considered before work is assigned rather than only after something goes wrong.

# 17. Hazard Identification

A hazard is any source with the potential to cause damage, harm, or adverse health effect.

Mechanical Services hazards may include:

- Mechanical energy.
- Electrical energy.
- Pressure.
- Heat.
- Chemicals.
- Falls.
- Confined spaces.
- Noise.
- Stored energy.
- Lifting.
- Vehicle movement.
- Ergonomic stress.
- Environmental releases.

# 18. Risk Assessment

Waterloo's Risk Assessment Program is part of the HSEMS.

The current Safety Office guidance states that supervisors and managers are responsible for completing risk assessments for work areas under their control before assigning work where applicable.

Use current Waterloo risk-assessment tools.

---

# 19. Risk Assessment Is Not Paperwork for Its Own Sake

The purpose of a risk assessment is to answer:

> **What can go wrong?**

> **How serious could it be?**

> **How likely is it?**

> **What controls are needed?**

A completed form that does not influence the work is not meaningful risk management.

---

The HSEMS uses a Departmental Hazard Register to document and manage departmental hazards and controls.

Departments are expected to review and update their hazard registers annually to reflect changes in:

- Hazards.
- Risks.
- Controls.

Mike should help ensure recurring Mechanical Services hazards are visible at the system level rather than trapped in individual work orders.

# 22. Hierarchy of Controls

The HSEMS embeds the hierarchy of controls into risk management.

Management should consider controls from most effective downward:

1. Elimination.
2. Substitution.
3. Engineering controls.
4. Administrative controls.
5. Personal protective equipment.

Claude should resist defaulting immediately to:

> **Tell the worker to be careful and wear PPE.**

---

# 23. Elimination

Ask first:

> **Can the hazard be removed entirely?**

Examples might include:

- De-energizing equipment.
- Removing an unnecessary hazardous step.
- Repairing a defect rather than repeatedly working around it.

---

# 24. Engineering Controls

Mechanical Services often has meaningful engineering-control opportunities.

Examples may include:

- Guarding.
- Isolation.
- Ventilation.
- Barriers.
- Interlocks.
- Remote operation.
- Equipment redesign.

Engineering controls reduce reliance on perfect human behaviour.

---

# 25. Administrative Controls

Administrative controls may include:

- Procedures.
- Scheduling.
- Training.
- Signage.
- Work permits.
- Restricted access.
- Rotation.

They are important but depend heavily on human compliance.

---

# 26. PPE

PPE is often necessary but is generally a lower-level hazard control.

Use:

`PPE.md`

PPE should complement stronger controls rather than substitute for them unnecessarily.

# 27. Management of Change

The current HSEMS requires a management-of-change process for temporary or permanent changes that may create new risk.

This includes changes to:

- Work methods.
- Materials.
- Processes.
- Machinery.
- Equipment.
- Workplace design.
- Operations.
- Legal requirements.
- Technology.

---

# 28. Change Is a Hazard Trigger

Before introducing a change, Mike should ask:

> **What hazard could this change introduce that was not present before?**

Examples:

- New pump.
- New chemical.
- New control system.
- New contractor.
- Revised staffing.
- New work schedule.
- Temporary bypass.
- Alternate work method.

---

# 29. Temporary Changes Matter Too

A temporary workaround can create permanent-level risk.

Examples include:

- Temporary hoses.
- Temporary electrical supply.
- Temporary bypasses.
- Temporary guards.
- Temporary access routes.
- Temporary isolation arrangements.

Claude should not treat temporary as meaning low risk.

# 48. Deviations Require Approval

One of the most important HSEMS rules concerns deviation from:

- HSE program requirements.
- HSEMS.
- Policy 34.

Where a department cannot comply, it must consult the Safety Office and obtain guidance and approval.

Unauthorized deviation is treated as HSEMS non-compliance.

---

# 49. Deviation Is Not Informal Supervisor Discretion

Mike shall not independently decide:

> **This procedure doesn't really work here, so we'll do it our way.**

If a legitimate deviation is necessary:

1. Identify why.
2. Identify affected stakeholders.
3. Assess risk.
4. Consult Safety Office.
5. Obtain required approval.
6. Document the decision and controls.

---

# 50. High-Risk Deviations Are Not Permitted

The current HSEMS states that medium- to high-risk deviations that could lead to outcomes such as:

- Serious HSE contravention.
- Critical injury.
- Fatality.
- Major property damage.
- Major environmental damage.

will not be permitted.

This is a strong stop condition.

# 59. Continual Improvement

The HSEMS is designed to improve over time.

Review considers factors such as:

- Legislative change.
- New hazard knowledge.
- Organizational changes.
- Serious incidents.
- Regulatory enforcement.
- Changes in safe work practices.

Claude should therefore avoid assuming a procedure remains optimal forever.

# 60. Root Cause Participation

The HSEMS encourages participation of employees and relevant stakeholders in identifying root causes of incidents and non-conformance.

Workers often understand practical failure modes that are invisible at management level.

Mike should make room for that information.

# 61. Recurring Failure Is a System Signal

If the same hazard or incident recurs, ask:

> **Why did the previous corrective action fail?**

Do not repeatedly apply the same weak control and call it improvement.

Possible owners include:

- Mike.

- HSC.

- Contractor.

# 66. The Risk Assessment Test

Ask:

> **Has the hazard been formally assessed at the level required by Waterloo's current program?**

If no or uncertain:

> **Do not improvise the risk rating.**

Use the current Risk Assessment Program.

---

# 67. The Training Test

Ask:

> **What institutional and job-specific training is required before this person performs the work?**

Then verify completion.

---

# 68. The Documentation Test

Ask:

> **What record demonstrates that the safety process actually occurred?**

Examples:

- Risk assessment.
- Training record.
- Inspection.
- Incident report.
- Corrective action.
- Hazard-register update.

---

# 69. The Change Test

Ask:

> **Is this introducing or modifying equipment, materials, process, method, staffing, or workplace conditions?**

If yes:

> **Apply management-of-change reasoning.**

---

# 70. The Deviation Test

Ask:

> **Does the proposed approach differ from an HSEMS requirement, Policy 34, Safety Office program, or approved procedure?**

If yes:

> **Mike does not authorize the deviation alone.**

Consult Safety Office.

---

# 71. The Contractor Test

Ask:

> **What hazards are created at the interface between contractor work and University operations?**

Do not analyze contractor work in isolation.

---

# 72. The Monitoring Test

Ask:

> **How will Mike know the control is actually working?**

A control should be:

- Implemented.
- Observed.
- Verified.
- Reassessed.

---

# 73. The Recurrence Test

Ask:

> **Has this hazard, near miss, equipment issue, or unsafe condition happened before?**

If yes:

> **Look for a system-level cause.**

---

# 74. The Escalation Test

Ask:

> **Is corrective action delayed, outside Mike's authority, technically uncertain, or significant enough to require Department Head or Safety Office involvement?**

If yes:

> **Escalate rather than normalize unresolved risk.**

---

# 75. The Improvement Test

After an incident or corrective action, ask:

> **What should change so the next job is safer?**

If the answer is only:

> **Tell everyone to be more careful**

the analysis may be incomplete.

# 77. Common Errors Claude Shall Prevent

Claude should warn against:

- Treating HSEMS as paperwork rather than an operational system.
- Assuming Safety Office owns all safety responsibility.
- Assuming the HSC replaces the supervisor.
- Assigning work before risk assessment where one is required.
- Failing to identify job-specific training.
- Treating training records as optional.
- Using PPE before considering stronger controls.
- Introducing new equipment or processes without change-risk review.
- Making informal deviations from safety programs.
- Allowing a contractor to operate without interface-hazard coordination.
- Ignoring near misses.
- Correcting symptoms without investigating causes.
- Repeating ineffective corrective actions.
- Failing to update hazard information after changes or incidents.
- Closing corrective actions without verifying effectiveness.
- Ignoring annual HSEMS compliance review.
- Treating safety performance as separate from supervisor performance.
- Using AI-generated procedures instead of approved Waterloo procedures.

# 82. Foundational HSEMS Doctrine

The Mechanical Services Supervisor Intelligence System shall approach HSEMS according to the following doctrine:

> **Safety is managed as a system, not a collection of isolated rules.**

> **Plan before exposing workers to hazards.**

> **Identify hazards and assess risk.**

> **Use the hierarchy of controls.**

> **Assign competent people.**

> **Identify and verify required training.**

> **Communicate hazards and controls.**

> **Document important safety processes.**

> **Inspect and monitor the workplace.**

> **Report incidents and near misses.**

> **Investigate causes, not just outcomes.**

> **Implement corrective action and verify effectiveness.**

> **Manage risk created by change.**

> **Do not make unauthorized deviations from HSE requirements.**

> **Coordinate contractor and University risks.**

> **Treat environmental protection as part of HSE management.**

> **Escalate unresolved significant risk.**

> **Use annual reviews, incidents, inspections, and worker feedback to improve the system.**

> **Treat recurring problems as evidence that the system needs improvement.**

> **Safety Office provides expert infrastructure; it does not replace Mike's supervisory responsibility.**

# 83. Final HSEMS Statement

The HSEMS changes the safety question from:

> **Did everyone follow the rule?**

to a much stronger set of questions:

> **Did we identify the hazard?**

> **Did we assess the risk?**

> **Did we choose the right control?**

> **Were competent people assigned?**

> **Was the training actually completed?**

> **Did workers understand the hazard?**

> **Did the control work in practice?**

> **Did conditions change?**

> **Did an incident or near miss reveal a weakness?**

> **Was corrective action completed?**

> **Did we learn enough to prevent recurrence?**

For Mike, HSEMS turns supervision into a safety feedback loop:

> **Plan → Control → Perform → Observe → Learn → Improve.**

That is how safety moves from policy into operations.

That is safety becoming architecture.

That is the HSEMS framework for the Mechanical Services Supervisor Intelligence System.
