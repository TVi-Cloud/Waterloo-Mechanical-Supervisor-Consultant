# 2. Core Principle

The foundational rule:

> **No person shall be exposed to hazardous energy that has not been properly identified, isolated, controlled, and verified.**

Shutdown is not the same as isolation.

Turning something off does not automatically make it safe.

---

# 3. What Is Hazardous Energy?

Hazardous energy is any energy source capable of causing injury when unexpectedly released.

Mechanical Services hazards may include:

- Electrical energy.
- Mechanical motion.
- Gravity.
- Hydraulic pressure.
- Pneumatic pressure.
- Steam pressure.
- Thermal energy.
- Chemical energy.
- Stored pressure.
- Springs or tension.
- Rotating equipment.
- Potential energy from elevated components.

---

# 4. Why Lockout Exists

Unexpected energization or release of stored energy can cause:

- Crushing.
- Amputation.
- Electrocution.
- Burns.
- Chemical exposure.
- Equipment movement.
- Pressure release.
- Serious injury or death.

The purpose of lockout is to prevent a worker from being exposed while performing servicing or maintenance.

# 5. Supervisor Responsibility

Mike's role is not only ensuring workers know lockout.

He must ensure:

- Proper procedures exist.
- Workers are trained.
- Energy sources are identified.
- Isolation is performed correctly.
- Verification occurs.
- Deviations are escalated.
- Contractors are coordinated.
- Unsafe work does not proceed.

---

# 6. Lockout Is a System

Effective energy control requires:

1. Identify energy sources.
2. Shut down equipment.
3. Isolate energy sources.
4. Apply locks/tags.
5. Dissipate stored energy.
6. Verify zero-energy state.
7. Perform work.
8. Restore service safely.

---

# 7. Identification Before Isolation

Before work begins, determine:

- What equipment is involved?
- What energy sources exist?
- Where are isolation points?
- What stored energy exists?
- Who is exposed?
- What procedure applies?

Do not begin isolation by guessing.

---

# 8. Zero Energy State

A machine being stopped does not mean it is safe.

Examples:

A pump may be stopped but still contain:

- Electrical energy.
- Pressure.
- Heat.
- Rotational energy.
- Chemical exposure.

The goal is:

> **A verified zero-energy state.**

---

# 9. Verification

Verification is one of the most important steps.

A proper isolation requires confirmation that energy has actually been controlled.

Verification may include:

- Attempted start.
- Meter testing where applicable.
- Pressure checks.
- Bleeding stored pressure.
- Visual confirmation.
- Mechanical restraint.

Never assume isolation worked.

---

# 10. Stored Energy

Stored energy must be released, restrained, blocked, or otherwise controlled.

Examples:

- Lower elevated components.
- Bleed pressure.
- Discharge capacitors.
- Block moving parts.
- Secure springs.
- Allow hot systems to cool.

---

Potential hazardous energy situations:

- Water systems under pressure.
- Rotating equipment.
- Motor-driven equipment.

---

# 12. Personal Locks

The core principle of personal lockout:

> **The person exposed to the hazard controls the lock protecting them.**

A worker should not rely only on another person's assurance.

---

# 13. Group Lockout

When multiple workers are involved:

- Each exposed worker must be protected.
- Each person's lock control must be respected.
- The system must ensure no worker is exposed before re-energization.

Group lockout requires a controlled process.

---

# 14. Contractor Coordination

Contractor involvement creates additional risks.

Before work begins:

- Confirm responsibilities.
- Confirm procedures.
- Confirm communication.
- Confirm isolation expectations.
- Confirm who controls energy sources.

A contractor's lockout process does not automatically replace Waterloo requirements.

---

# 15. Lock Removal

A lock should normally only be removed by the person who applied it.

If exceptional circumstances require removal:

- Follow the approved procedure.
- Confirm the worker's status.
- Attempt contact.
- Verify safety.
- Document the event.

Never casually remove another person's lock.

---

# 16. Troubleshooting and Testing

Troubleshooting creates special risk because workers may need equipment energized.

Before allowing energized troubleshooting:

Ask:

- Is it permitted?
- Is there an approved procedure?
- Are qualified people involved?
- Are required controls present?
- Is exposure minimized?

Do not bypass lockout casually because diagnosis is inconvenient.

---

# 17. Maintenance Versus Production

Operational pressure is not a reason to defeat energy control.

The question is not:

> "How quickly can we get running?"

The question is:

> "How can we restore operation without exposing people to uncontrolled energy?"

---

# 18. Training Requirements

Workers performing lockout-related work require appropriate:

- Hazard awareness.
- Lockout procedure training.
- Equipment-specific instruction.
- Practical competency verification.

Training must match the actual hazards.

---

# 19. Competency Model

Use:

> READ → WATCH → PERFORM → VERIFY

READ:
- Procedure.
- Hazard assessment.
- Equipment information.

WATCH:
- Competent demonstration.

PERFORM:
- Worker completes task under supervision.

VERIFY:
- Competence confirmed.

Claude should recognize these warning signs:

- "It's only going to take five minutes."
- "The switch is off."
- "Nobody will turn it on."
- "We've always done it this way."
- "The pressure should be gone."
- "The contractor handles that."
- "We don't need to lock this one."

# 21. Incident Prevention Questions

Before work begins:

- What can move?
- What can release pressure?
- What can energize?
- What can fall?
- What can burn?
- What can expose someone to chemicals?
- How do we prove it cannot happen?

---

# 22. Restart Process

Before re-energization:

Verify:

- Work is complete.
- Tools are removed.
- Guards are restored.
- Workers are clear.
- Locks are removed according to procedure.
- Communication is complete.
- Equipment can safely return to service.

# 24. Hierarchy of Controls

Lockout is an administrative/control procedure.

Where possible, also consider:

- Elimination.
- Engineering controls.
- Equipment design.
- Guarding.
- Automation.
- Physical isolation.

The best hazard is one that cannot exist.

# 28. Common Errors Claude Shall Prevent

Claude should warn against:

- Treating shutdown as isolation.
- Skipping verification.
- Allowing workers to rely on verbal assurances.
- Removing locks casually.
- Ignoring stored energy.
- Allowing production pressure to override safety.
- Assuming contractors remove responsibility.
- Allowing untrained workers to perform lockout.
- Using generic procedures where equipment-specific procedures exist.
- Restarting equipment without confirming worker safety.
- Treating lockout as paperwork instead of hazard control.

---

# 29. Foundational Hazardous Energy Doctrine

> **Energy control is a life-protection system.**

> **Every energy source must be identified.**

> **Every energy source must be controlled.**

> **Every isolation must be verified.**

> **Every exposed worker must be protected.**

> **Every deviation requires careful escalation.**

> **Every restart requires confirmation.**

> **No deadline, production goal, or convenience justifies uncontrolled energy exposure.**

> **The purpose of lockout is not to slow work down. It is to ensure workers go home.**

# 30. Final Statement

For Mechanical Services, hazardous energy control is one of the clearest examples of values becoming architecture:

- Safety first becomes isolation.
- Accountability becomes verification.
- Communication becomes coordination.
- Integrity becomes refusing shortcuts.
- Leadership becomes stopping unsafe work.

The question is never:

> "Can we make this happen faster?"

The question is:

> "Can we make this happen safely, with confidence that every person exposed is protected?"

That is hazardous energy control becoming architecture.
