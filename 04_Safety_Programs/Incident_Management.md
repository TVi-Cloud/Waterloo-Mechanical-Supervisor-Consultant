# 6. What Is an Incident?

The current Waterloo program defines an incident as an unwanted and unplanned event that may or does result in:

- Injury.
- Illness.
- Equipment damage.
- Property damage.

The intelligence system should also recognize related events such as:

- Near misses.
- Occupational illness.
- Hazardous releases.
- Fires.
- Serious equipment failures.
- Safety-system failures.

---

# 7. Near Miss

A near miss is a work-related condition, incident, or unplanned event that did not result in injury, illness, equipment damage, or property damage but had the potential to do so.

Examples in Mechanical Services may include:

- A suspended load nearly falling.
- Unexpected pressure release without injury.
- Equipment unexpectedly energizing while no worker is exposed.
- A worker nearly falling.
- A tool dropped from height without striking anyone.
- A lockout defect discovered before work begins.

Near misses should be treated as valuable warning signals.

---

# 8. Hazard Versus Incident

A hazard is a source of potential harm.

An incident is an event.

Example:

> **Exposed rotating equipment = hazard.**

> **Worker's clothing is caught by exposed rotating equipment = incident.**

A hazard may be reported before an incident occurs.

That is preferable.

# 21. Fatality or Critical Injury Response

The current Waterloo program directs that, after emergency services and UW Special Constables are contacted:

- The incident scene is cordoned off.
- The supervisor works with UW Special Constables.
- The scene is preserved.
- The Safety Office is immediately notified.

- The Safety Office participates in the investigation.

# 22. Scene Preservation

Ontario law may restrict disturbance of a scene following a fatality or critical injury.

Waterloo's current program states that the supervisor should ensure:

> **No part of the scene is disturbed and no materials are removed until permission is given by the appropriate Ministry inspector.**

There are legal exceptions, including circumstances necessary to:

- Save life.
- Relieve human suffering.
- Maintain essential public utility/service.
- Prevent unnecessary equipment or property damage.

# 26. Investigation Objective

The current Waterloo program states that incident investigations are intended to:

- Evaluate severity.
- Assess recurrence potential.
- Determine contributing conditions and practices.
- Communicate cause information.
- Identify corrective controls.
- Analyze trends.
- Identify hazards.
- Improve risk control.

The investigation is not simply:

> **Who screwed up?**

# 27. Immediate Cause

The immediate cause is the condition or action directly associated with the event.

Examples:

- Guard missing.
- Valve opened unexpectedly.
- Worker slips.
- Equipment starts.
- Load shifts.
- Tool fails.

Stopping at the immediate cause often produces weak corrective action.

---

# 28. Root Cause

Root causes explain why the immediate cause existed.

Potential root causes may involve:

- Inadequate procedure.
- Poor equipment design.
- Training deficiency.
- Inadequate supervision.
- Maintenance failure.
- Communication breakdown.
- Scheduling pressure.
- Incorrect risk assessment.
- Unauthorized modification.
- Weak contractor coordination.

Root-cause analysis should look beyond the final human action.

---

# 29. Human Error Is Not Usually the End of Analysis

If the investigation concludes:

> **Employee made a mistake.**

Claude should ask:

> **Why was one human mistake enough to create this consequence?**

Possible additional questions:

- Was there a guard?
- Was isolation verified?
- Was the task designed safely?
- Was training adequate?
- Was supervision adequate?
- Was the procedure usable?
- Was fatigue involved?
- Was there time pressure?

---

# 30. Blame Versus Accountability

Incident investigation should not become blame avoidance.

Workers can still be accountable for conduct.

Supervisors can still be accountable for failures.

The purpose is to identify:

> **What must change to prevent recurrence?**

Employment discipline, if appropriate, is a separate process.

# 33. Interviewing Witnesses

Good incident interviews should focus on:

- What the person saw.
- What they heard.
- What they did.
- What happened immediately before.
- What happened immediately after.
- What conditions existed.

Avoid leading questions.

Do not pressure witnesses to agree with a preferred theory.

---

# 34. Separate Witness Accounts

Where practical, obtain witness accounts separately before group discussion influences memory.

Memory can change after witnesses talk to each other.

Claude should encourage factual, non-leading interviews.

---

# 35. Ask Open Questions

Useful questions include:

- What happened?
- What were you doing immediately before?
- What did you observe?
- What equipment was involved?
- What controls were in place?
- What was different from normal?
- Was anything unusual?
- What do you think could prevent this happening again?

---

# 36. Avoid Accusatory Interviewing

Poor questions include:

- Why did you ignore the rule?
- You knew better, didn't you?
- Who is responsible for this?
- Why were you careless?

Those questions presume conclusions.

Investigation should establish facts first.

# 40. Corrective Action

The Waterloo program requires supervisors to implement corrective action to eliminate or reduce hazards.

Corrective action should be specific.

Weak:

> **Workers reminded to be careful.**

Stronger:

> **Install fixed guard, revise isolation procedure, retrain affected workers, and verify implementation.**

---

# 41. Hierarchy of Controls

Incident corrective action should use the hierarchy of controls:

1. Elimination.
2. Substitution.
3. Engineering controls.
4. Administrative controls.
5. PPE.

Training alone should not be the default response when stronger controls are feasible.

---

# 42. Corrective Action Ownership

Every corrective action should identify:

- What must be done.
- Who owns it.
- Target completion date.
- Interim controls.
- How completion will be verified.

An action without ownership often becomes an unresolved hazard.

---

# 43. Interim Controls

Permanent corrective action may take time.

Until then, Mike may need:

- Work stoppage.
- Restricted access.
- Temporary isolation.
- Alternate procedure.
- Increased supervision.
- Temporary engineering controls.

Interim control must actually reduce risk.

# 65. The Immediate Danger Test

Ask:

> **Could anyone be injured if conditions remain as they are right now?**

If yes:

> **Control the hazard before paperwork.**

---

# 66. The Medical Test

Ask:

> **Does anyone need immediate first aid, emergency response, or professional medical treatment?**

If uncertain and symptoms are potentially serious:

> **Seek appropriate medical assistance.**

---

# 67. The Critical-Injury Test

Ask:

> **Could this event meet Ontario's current legal definition of fatality or critical injury?**

If yes or uncertain:

> **Immediately escalate through the Safety Office / UW emergency process and verify current law.**

---

# 68. The Scene-Preservation Test

Ask:

> **Could legal scene-preservation requirements apply?**

If yes:

> **Do not disturb the scene except as legally permitted for immediate safety and other statutory exceptions.**

---

# 69. The Reportability Test

Ask:

> **What University, WSIB, OHSA, contractor, or other reporting requirement applies?**

Do not rely on one reporting system to automatically satisfy every legal obligation.

---

# 70. The Evidence Test

Ask:

> **What evidence could disappear if we repair, clean, restart, move equipment, or allow normal work to resume?**

Preserve what is relevant.

---

# 71. The Cause Test

Ask:

> **Why did this event become possible?**

If the answer is only:

> **Worker error**

continue the analysis.

---

# 72. The Control Test

Ask:

> **What control would actually prevent or substantially reduce recurrence?**

Prefer higher-level controls.

---

# 73. The Completion Test

Ask:

> **Has the corrective action actually been completed?**

A planned action is not a completed action.

---

# 74. The Effectiveness Test

Ask:

> **How do we know the corrective action works?**

Verification may include:

- Inspection.
- Observation.
- Test.
- Audit.
- Worker feedback.
- Absence of recurrence over time.

---

# 75. The Trend Test

Ask:

> **Has anything similar happened before?**

If yes:

> **Treat the pattern as a system signal.**

---

# 76. The Restart Test

Ask:

> **What condition must be satisfied before work or equipment safely resumes?**

If the answer is unclear:

> **Do not restart yet.**

# 78. Common Errors Claude Shall Prevent

Claude should warn against:

- Prioritizing paperwork over emergency care.
- Failing to prevent further injury.
- Delaying the initial incident report while waiting for investigation completion.
- Failing to update the report after new medical treatment.
- Treating near misses as unimportant.
- Assuming no injury means low potential.
- Repairing or cleaning a serious incident scene before clearance.
- Restarting equipment too quickly.
- Blaming a worker before completing causal analysis.
- Ending root-cause analysis at "human error."
- Using retraining as the only corrective action automatically.
- Failing to assign corrective-action ownership.
- Closing corrective action without verification.
- Suppressing incident reporting to protect safety metrics.
- Discouraging WSIB reporting.
- Collecting unnecessary medical information.
- Treating contractor incidents as irrelevant to Waterloo.
- Mixing safety investigation and discipline without process separation.
- Ignoring repeated minor incidents.
- Uploading sensitive incident records into unauthorized AI systems.
- Using AI as the official incident investigator or legal reporting authority.

# 83. Foundational Incident Management Doctrine

The Mechanical Services Supervisor Intelligence System shall approach incidents according to the following doctrine:

> **Protect people first.**

> **Prevent the second injury.**

> **Obtain first aid and emergency medical assistance promptly.**

> **Report incidents and near misses promptly.**

> **Do not delay initial reporting while seeking perfect information.**

> **Update reports when facts or medical status change.**

> **Treat potential critical injuries as immediate escalation events.**

> **Preserve serious incident scenes as required by law.**

> **Do not repair away the evidence.**

> **Investigate every reported incident in a timely manner.**

> **Separate immediate causes from root causes.**

> **Do not stop at human error.**

> **Use evidence, not assumptions.**

> **Give witnesses room to describe what they actually observed.**

> **Use the hierarchy of controls for corrective action.**

> **Assign ownership and completion dates.**

> **Verify corrective-action effectiveness.**

> **Do not restart work merely because operations are waiting.**

> **Treat near misses as free warnings.**

> **Treat recurring minor incidents as system signals.**

> **Separate safety investigation from employment discipline while coordinating both appropriately.**

> **Protect medical and personal information.**

> **Use incident learning to improve risk assessments, SOPs, training, inspections, and the Hazard Register.**

> **AI can organize incident reasoning; it cannot replace emergency responders, Safety Office, legal reporting, investigators, engineers, medical professionals, or regulatory authorities.**

# 84. Final Incident Management Statement

The quality of incident management is determined less by the form that gets filed and more by what happens after something goes wrong.

Mike's first questions should be:

> **Is anyone hurt?**

> **Can anyone else get hurt?**

> **Do we need emergency help?**

> **Do we need to preserve this scene?**

> **Who needs to know immediately?**

Then:

> **What actually happened?**

> **What made it possible?**

> **What evidence supports that conclusion?**

> **What control failed?**

> **What control would prevent recurrence?**

> **Who owns the corrective action?**

> **How will we know it worked?**

> **What should the rest of Mechanical Services learn from this?**

A near miss is information.

An incident is information bought at a higher price.

A serious injury is information bought at a price no organization wants to pay.

The responsibility of a strong safety system is to learn from each signal before the next consequence is worse.

That is incident management becoming architecture.

That is the Incident Management framework for the Mechanical Services Supervisor Intelligence System.
