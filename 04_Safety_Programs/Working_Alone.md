# 2. Core Principle

The foundational rule:

> **A worker who is alone must have a level of protection appropriate to the hazards they face and the ability to obtain assistance when needed.**

Working alone is not automatically unsafe.

The risk depends on:

- Task.
- Environment.
- Equipment.
- Hazards.
- Available communication.
- Emergency response capability.

---

# 3. What Is Working Alone?

Working alone occurs when a worker performs duties where:

- They are isolated from other workers.
- Immediate assistance may not be available.
- Communication or emergency response may be delayed.

Examples:

- After-hours maintenance.
- Mechanical rooms.
- Remote campus areas.
- Call-ins.
- Emergency repairs.

---

# 4. Supervisor Responsibility

Mike's responsibilities include:

- Identifying when workers may be alone.
- Assessing associated hazards.
- Establishing appropriate controls.
- Ensuring procedures are followed.
- Confirming communication methods.
- Reviewing incidents and near misses.

---

# 5. Risk Assessment

Before allowing working alone, consider:

- What task is being performed?
- What hazards exist?
- Could the worker become incapacitated?
- How quickly could help arrive?
- What communication exists?
- What emergency procedures apply?

---

# 6. High-Risk Alone Work

Working alone requires greater caution when involving:

- Hazardous energy.
- Confined spaces.
- Hot work.
- Heights.
- Chemicals.
- Heavy equipment.
- Emergency repairs.
- Severe environments.

Some tasks may require that workers are not alone.

---

# 7. The Assistance Question

Ask:

> **If something goes wrong, how will this worker get help?**

If the answer is unclear:

> **The work arrangement requires reassessment.**

---

# 8. Communication Controls

Potential controls include:

- Radio communication.
- Phone communication.
- Check-in systems.
- Scheduled contact.
- Monitoring systems.
- Supervisor awareness.

The chosen method must match the hazard.

---

# 9. Check-In Systems

A check-in process should define:

- Who checks in?
- When check-ins occur?
- What happens if contact is missed?
- Who responds?
- How escalation occurs?

A check-in system is only useful if someone acts when a check-in fails.

---

# 10. Emergency Response

Consider:

- Emergency contacts.
- Location information.
- Access routes.
- Campus emergency procedures.
- First aid availability.
- Rescue capability.

# 14. After-Hours Work

After-hours maintenance may introduce:

- Reduced staffing.
- Longer emergency response times.
- Fewer witnesses.
- Reduced support availability.

The task should be evaluated differently from normal daytime work.

---

# 15. Emergency Callouts

Before assigning emergency work:

Consider:

- Is the worker competent?
- Is the hazard controlled?
- Is another person required?
- Is the urgency real?
- Can the work safely wait?

Urgency does not eliminate hazard assessment.

---

# 16. Worker Fitness

Working alone requires awareness of:

- Fatigue.
- Stress.
- Impairment.
- Physical limitations.
- Familiarity with the task.

A worker should not be assigned alone when circumstances create unreasonable risk.

---

# 17. Training and Competence

Workers performing alone must have:

- Appropriate training.
- Task competence.
- Emergency knowledge.
- Understanding of procedures.

A new worker may require additional supervision before independent work.

---

# 18. Incident Connection

If a working-alone incident occurs:

1. Protect the worker.
2. Obtain assistance.
3. Report the incident.
4. Investigate contributing factors.
5. Review whether the working arrangement was appropriate.

## Review

---

# 20. Stop Work Triggers

Stop or modify working-alone arrangements if:

- Communication fails.
- Conditions change.
- Hazards increase.
- Emergency response is unavailable.
- Worker competence is uncertain.
- Required controls are missing.

---

# 21. Common Errors Claude Shall Prevent

Claude should warn against:

- Assuming working alone is automatically acceptable.
- Assuming a phone solves every emergency.
- Sending inexperienced workers alone.
- Ignoring delayed emergency response.
- Treating check-ins as paperwork.
- Allowing lone workers to perform tasks requiring multiple people.
- Ignoring fatigue or changing conditions.
- Letting urgency override risk assessment.

---

# 22. Foundational Working-Alone Doctrine

> **A worker alone must never be forgotten by the system.**

> **The farther away help is, the stronger the planning must be.**

> **Communication is a control, not a guarantee.**

> **The best emergency response is preventing the emergency.**

> **Tasks must match the worker's competence and available support.**

> **Working alone is a risk-management decision, not simply a scheduling decision.**

---

# 23. Final Statement

Working-alone supervision represents the responsibility to protect workers when normal human backup is removed.

Mike's questions:

> **Who is alone?**

> **Where are they?**

> **What could happen?**

> **How would they get help?**

> **Who would know if something went wrong?**

> **Should this work be performed alone at all?**

Safety becomes architecture when every worker remains connected to the safety system, even when nobody else is physically nearby.
