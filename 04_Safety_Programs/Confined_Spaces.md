# 2. Core Principle

The foundational rule:

> **No person enters a confined space unless the hazards have been assessed, controls are established, and the entry is authorized according to the applicable procedure.**

A confined space is not dangerous because of its size.

It is dangerous because normal assumptions about a workplace may no longer apply.

---

# 3. What Is a Confined Space?

A confined space is generally a fully or partially enclosed space that:

- Is not designed or intended for continuous human occupancy.
- Has restricted access or egress.
- May become hazardous due to its design, atmosphere, contents, or work activity.

Examples may include:

- Tanks.
- Vessels.
- Pits.
- Vaults.
- Sewers.
- Manholes.
- Ducts.
- Certain mechanical spaces.

Always verify the current legal definition and Waterloo procedure.

---

# 4. Why Confined Spaces Are High Risk

Confined spaces may contain hazards that are not obvious.

Potential hazards include:

- Oxygen deficiency.
- Toxic gases.
- Flammable atmospheres.
- Engulfment.
- Stored energy.
- Unexpected equipment operation.
- Heat stress.
- Limited rescue access.
- Chemical exposure.

# 6. The "No Improvised Entry" Rule

Claude should recognize warning statements:

- "It's only a quick look."
- "We don't need a permit."
- "The air should be fine."
- "Someone can just climb in."
- "We've done it before."

# 7. Hazard Assessment Before Entry

Before entry, identify:

- Space hazards.
- Atmospheric hazards.
- Energy sources.
- Chemical hazards.
- Physical hazards.
- Work activity hazards.
- Rescue requirements.

The assessment must reflect the actual conditions.

---

# 8. Atmospheric Hazards

Potential atmospheric hazards include:

## Oxygen Deficiency

Insufficient oxygen can quickly become fatal.

## Oxygen Enrichment

Increased oxygen can increase fire risk.

## Toxic Atmospheres

Examples may include:

- Hydrogen sulfide.
- Carbon monoxide.
- Chemical vapours.

## Flammable Atmospheres

Combustible gases or vapours may create explosion risk.

---

# 9. Atmospheric Testing

Where required, atmospheric testing should verify conditions before entry.

Testing may involve:

- Oxygen.
- Flammable gases.
- Toxic gases.

Testing equipment must be:

- Appropriate.
- Maintained.
- Calibrated as required.
- Used by trained personnel.

---

# 10. Testing Is Not a One-Time Guarantee

Conditions can change.

A safe atmosphere at entry does not guarantee safety throughout the job.

Consider:

- Continuous monitoring.
- Work activity changes.
- Ventilation changes.
- Chemical releases.
- Equipment changes.

---

# 11. Ventilation

Ventilation may be required to control atmospheric hazards.

However:

> **Ventilation does not replace hazard assessment, testing, or other required controls.**

# 14. Training and Competence

Confined-space work requires appropriate training.

Training must match:

- Role.
- Hazard.
- Equipment.
- Rescue responsibilities.

A person trained for one confined-space task may not automatically be competent for another.

---

# 15. Roles in Confined-Space Work

Depending on the procedure, roles may include:

- Entrant.
- Attendant.
- Entry supervisor.
- Rescue personnel.

Each role has specific responsibilities.

---

# 16. Attendant Role

An attendant is not simply a person standing nearby.

The attendant may need to:

- Monitor entrants.
- Maintain communication.
- Recognize hazards.
- Initiate emergency response.
- Prevent unauthorized entry.

---

# 17. Rescue Planning

A rescue plan must exist before entry.

Ask:

- How will the worker be removed?
- Who performs rescue?
- What equipment is required?
- Is rescue internal or external?
- Can emergency responders reach the location?

---

# 18. Never Create a Second Victim

A common confined-space fatality pattern is:

1. Worker enters.
2. Worker becomes incapacitated.
3. Another person enters without protection.
4. Second person is also harmed.

The rule:

> **Do not perform an unplanned rescue.**

---

Contractor confined-space work requires coordination.

# 20. Incident Response

If a confined-space incident occurs:

1. Do not create additional victims.
2. Activate emergency response.
3. Follow rescue procedures.
4. Protect others from entry.
5. Report and investigate.

# 23. Stop Work Triggers

Stop work if:

- Atmospheric conditions become unsafe.
- Required equipment fails.
- Communication is lost.
- Workers are not trained.
- Rescue capability is unavailable.
- Conditions change significantly.
- Required controls are missing.

---

# 24. Common Errors Claude Shall Prevent

Claude should warn against:

- Treating a confined space as a normal workspace.
- Entering "just for a minute."
- Skipping atmospheric testing.
- Assuming ventilation is enough.
- Entering without rescue planning.
- Allowing untrained workers to enter.
- Performing improvised rescues.
- Ignoring energy isolation.
- Assuming contractors remove University responsibility.
- Restarting work after changing conditions without reassessment.

---

# 25. Foundational Confined-Space Doctrine

> **A confined space is a controlled environment, not a normal workplace.**

> **No entry occurs without hazard assessment.**

> **No entry occurs without appropriate training and authorization.**

> **Atmospheric conditions must be verified.**

> **Energy sources must be controlled.**

> **Rescue must be planned before entry, not invented during an emergency.**

> **The safest rescue is preventing the emergency.**

> **Production pressure never justifies uncontrolled confined-space entry.**

---

# 26. Final Statement

Confined-space safety represents the highest level of supervisory responsibility because mistakes can become fatal quickly.

Mike's key questions:

> **Is this a confined space?**

> **What can hurt someone here?**

> **How do we know the atmosphere is safe?**

> **How do we prevent unexpected energy release?**

> **Who is trained and authorized?**

> **How do we rescue someone if everything goes wrong?**

> **Should this work proceed today?**

Safety becomes architecture when the system prevents the emergency before the emergency happens.
