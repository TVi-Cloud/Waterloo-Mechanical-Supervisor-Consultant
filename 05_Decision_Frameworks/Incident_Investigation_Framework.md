# 1. Purpose

This document provides Claude with a structured framework for supporting
Mike when reviewing workplace incidents, near misses, hazards, or
failures.

It is designed to help with:

-   Understanding what happened.
-   Gathering facts.
-   Identifying contributing factors.
-   Determining corrective actions.
-   Preventing recurrence.
-   Supporting a learning culture.

This framework does not replace formal University investigations, Safety
Office processes, regulatory requirements, or authorized investigation
procedures.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 2. Core Principle

The foundational rule:

> **The purpose of an investigation is not to find someone to blame. The
> purpose is to understand what happened and improve the system.**

A strong investigation asks:

> "How did the system allow this to happen?"

Not only:

> "Who made the mistake?"

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 3. Investigation Priorities

The investigation sequence:

``` text
Protect people
      |
      ▼
Preserve facts
      |
      ▼
Understand events
      |
      ▼
Identify contributing factors
      |
      ▼
Develop corrective actions
      |
      ▼
Verify improvement
```

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 4. Immediate Response Comes First

Before investigating:

Confirm:

-   Injured persons are supported.
-   Hazards are controlled.
-   Further exposure is prevented.
-   Required notifications are made.

Use:

`Immediate_Safety_Event_Framework.md`

# 5. Facts Before Conclusions

A strong investigation separates:

## Facts

-   What was observed.
-   What was documented.
-   What occurred.

## Assumptions

-   Why someone acted.
-   What someone intended.
-   Personal opinions.

Investigations should be evidence-based.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 6. Investigation Questions

Ask:

## What happened?

Describe the event.

## When and where did it happen?

Establish timeline and location.

## Who was involved?

Identify people, equipment, and systems.

## What conditions existed?

Consider:

-   Environment.
-   Equipment.
-   Procedures.
-   Communication.
-   Workload.

## Why did existing controls fail?

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 7. The Event Timeline

Build understanding by reviewing:

-   Before the event.
-   During the event.
-   After the event.

Consider:

-   Instructions provided.
-   Actions taken.
-   Equipment condition.
-   Communication.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 8. Root Cause Thinking

Avoid stopping at:

> "The worker made a mistake."

Ask deeper questions:

-   Was training adequate?
-   Were procedures clear?
-   Was equipment appropriate?
-   Were expectations realistic?
-   Were hazards identified?
-   Were controls effective?

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 9. Human Factors

Consider:

-   Training.
-   Experience.
-   Communication.
-   Fatigue.
-   Work pressure.
-   Environmental conditions.
-   Procedure design.

Human error is often a symptom, not the entire cause.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 10. Corrective Actions

Strong corrective actions improve systems.

Examples:

-   Remove hazards.
-   Improve procedures.
-   Provide training.
-   Modify equipment.
-   Improve communication.
-   Update inspections.

Weak corrective action:

> "Be more careful."

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 11. Near Miss Investigations

Near misses deserve attention.

A near miss means:

> The system allowed a dangerous condition to exist without causing harm
> this time.

The goal:

Prevent the next occurrence.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 12. Mechanical Services Examples

Potential investigations:

-   Equipment failure.
-   Unexpected energy release.
-   Water leak.
-   Mechanical breakdown.
-   Unsafe maintenance activity.
-   Contractor issue.
-   Work quality concern.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 13. Investigation and Accountability

Understanding causes does not eliminate accountability.

A fair investigation determines:

-   What happened.
-   Why it happened.
-   Whether expectations were met.
-   What response is appropriate.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 14. Documentation Standards

Record:

-   Facts.
-   Evidence.
-   Interviews or discussions.
-   Timeline.
-   Findings.
-   Corrective actions.

Avoid:

-   Blame language.
-   Personal judgments.
-   Unsupported conclusions.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 15. Follow-Up and Verification

An investigation is incomplete until improvements are checked.

Ask:

-   Was the corrective action completed?
-   Did it reduce risk?
-   Did the problem return?
-   What was learned?

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 16. Common Investigation Errors

Claude should prevent:

## Blame First Thinking

Finding fault before understanding.

## Stopping at Human Error

Ignoring system factors.

## Weak Corrective Actions

Creating reminders instead of improvements.

## Failing to Follow Up

Assuming a fix worked.

## Ignoring Near Misses

Missing opportunities to learn.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 18. Foundational Investigation Doctrine

> **Investigations are for learning, not blame.**

> **Every incident is a question the system must answer.**

> **Facts create understanding.**

> **Root causes create improvement.**

> **The best investigation prevents the next incident.**

> **Accountability and learning can exist together.**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 19. Final Statement

An incident is not the end of a safety process.

It is information that allows the organization to become better.

Mike's questions should be:

> **What happened?**

> **Why did it happen?**

> **What controls failed?**

> **What can we improve?**

> **How do we know the improvement worked?**

Safety becomes architecture when every event becomes an opportunity to
strengthen the system.
